# AI in recruitment

## Question

A national retailer receives over 80,000 graduate-role applications a
year. The retailer adopts an AI screening tool that scores each
application against criteria derived from the past five years of
successful hires, and gives recruiters a ranked shortlist.

Discuss the potential impacts on the retailer of using the AI tool to
rank applications.

**[6 marks]**

## Model answer

One positive impact for the retailer is the operational efficiency of
automated CV screening at scale. Because the AI can sift through tens
of thousands of applications in minutes against the same scoring
criteria, the recruitment team is freed from the most time-consuming
part of the early funnel and is no longer the bottleneck. This lets
recruiters spend more time on shortlisted-candidate engagement,
reduces the time-to-hire, and produces a more consistent applicant
experience for those who progress, all of which improve the retailer's
competitiveness for talent.

One negative impact is the risk of indirect discrimination against
applicants in protected groups. AI trained on the retailer's
historical hiring data inherits the patterns of who has been hired
before, so any historical preference for one demographic over another,
even one that was unintentional, shows up as a learned scoring rule
that disadvantages similar candidates today. This creates simultaneous
exposure under two Acts. Under the Equality Act 2010 it is likely to
be indirect discrimination under section 19, because the screening
rule applies uniformly but puts protected groups at a particular
disadvantage. Under the UK GDPR, Article 22 restricts solely automated
decisions producing legal or similarly significant effects on data
subjects, requiring safeguards such as meaningful human review and a
right to challenge the outcome.

---

## Notes on calibration

This is a two-Act scenario by design. The Equality Act is the primary
tier because the harm is discriminatory ranking on a protected
characteristic. UK GDPR Article 22 is the secondary tier because the
ranking is a solely-automated decision producing significant effects.
A student who names only one Act loses the cross-tier mark; a student
who names both and ties each to the right harm earns it.

The scenario deliberately puts the multi-Act exposure in the
develop slot, not in the introduce slot. The introduce slot is a
single clean "what" claim (indirect discrimination risk). The develop
slot is where the statutory analysis lives. This mirrors the way
examiner mark schemes credit a clean headline impact in the topic
sentence and richer statutory engagement in the supporting prose.

The positive track is deliberately single-tier (operational
efficiency). Adding a regulatory positive (e.g., the retailer can
document its scoring criteria for ICO accountability purposes) would
muddle the contrast between the tracks. Twin Tracks is a recognition
exercise; clean contrast helps the student internalise the shape.
